This capstone pulls together everything in the course: the memory model from Module 1 (the
forgetting curve, encoding vs. retrieval) and the four techniques from Module 2 (spacing,
interleaving, retrieval practice, elaboration). You'll design a real, runnable plan — then judge
it against the rubric below.

## The task

Pick something concrete you genuinely need to learn over the **next two weeks** (a chapter, a
skill, a certification section — not "get better at X" in the abstract). Design a two-week study
plan for it and write it down. Your plan must:

1. **Name the material and a testable goal.** What will you be able to *do* at the end that you
   can't now? State it as an observable outcome, not "understand it better."
2. **Schedule with expanding intervals.** Lay out at least four study touches across the two
   weeks. The gaps between touches should grow (e.g. day 1 → day 3 → day 7 → day 13), not sit at
   a fixed daily cadence.
3. **Make every touch retrieval, not re-reading.** For each touch, write the specific retrieval
   action — blank-page recall, self-quiz, redo a problem from memory, teach it aloud. "Re-read
   the notes" is the failure mode this course exists to kill.
4. **Interleave at least once.** At one late touch, mix this material with a related topic
   instead of blocking on it alone, and say why that helps.
5. **Add one elaboration prompt.** Write a "why / how does this connect to what I already know?"
   question you'll answer from memory at least once.

Write the plan out fully before you read the notes below — producing it from memory *is* the
retrieval practice.

## What a strong plan looks like

A strong plan reads like a calendar you could actually follow tomorrow: dated touches with
expanding gaps, a concrete retrieval action on each (not a vague "review"), one interleaved
session near the end, and an elaboration question tied to prior knowledge. A weak plan hides
re-reading inside words like "go over" or "look at again," uses fixed daily intervals, or states
a fuzzy goal you couldn't test.

## Self-assess

Score your plan against the rubric on this page — one level per criterion. Be honest: the point
isn't a grade, it's spotting which technique you left out so you can fix the plan before you run
it. If any criterion lands at the lowest level, revise that part now.
